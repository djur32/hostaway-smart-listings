Hostaway's help centreThe full map

We started by mapping property management end to end. We used a Hostaway trial account, with a real Airbnb listing in Berlin. Not the whole product, just the part where a host sets up and runs a property. Everything hangs off the property: ten sections, in the product’s own menu order, from Overview to Direct.

[Press] The listing, the thing a guest reads, isn’t one of those sections. Each connected channel, like Airbnb, Booking.com or Direct, is a row on Overview. None gets a page of its own.

[Press] What can differ per channel lives inside the sections. Details, Pricing, Availability, Booking policies and Guest experience each have their own channel settings, field by field.

[Press] So each channel’s listing gets its own pages, with tips on every channel. The full map, every section with its pages, and how we made it, are in the detail. We mapped the real product first because a concept built on a guess of the structure would have nothing to stand on. The constraint is one account, so we read it as the structure of the product and not as a sample of hosts. About 40 seconds.

This is the slide the concept rests on. So we made sure it’s true for any host, not just our trial account. Next to every listing, Hostaway keeps a Knowledge Hub. Today it repeats the listing. The amenities, the rules and check-in are written again as separate entries, each with one source and a date. In our heuristic evaluation, that breaks source-of-truth clarity, at severity 4.

[Press] The Hub is filled once, when the host presses Generate, from Details and past messages. Hostaway’s help centre says it reads Details only once. So later edits never reach it. It drifts by design, not by accident.

[Press] And the assistant answers from that copy, not from the listing. On our trial listing, Ask the AI told a guest the apartment is wheelchair accessible. No amenity says so, and no source is shown. That breaks AI transparency, also a 4.

[Press] Meanwhile, the questions guests really ask stay in the Inbox, and nothing learns from them. So the Hub has no job of its own. Our answer: listing details live once, in the Listing base. The Hub reads them, adds what the listing can’t say, and its job becomes guests’ questions. The severities are our own judgement, from one evaluator. About 60 seconds.

DiscoveryHeuristic evaluation

No screen shows one listing on one channel

Hostaway keeps per-channel values field by field, never as one listing.

SoEach field saves on its own: a change reaches the channels that read it. A custom value stays on its channel.

Product auditSeverity 0 to 4, one evaluator

The second gap in the structure is the channel view. Say a host asks the simplest question: what does our Airbnb listing say right now?

[Press] To answer it, they hop across five sections: Details, Pricing, Availability, Booking policies and Direct. They check about fifteen fields, one info icon at a time. And at the end, no page shows the answer. In the heuristic evaluation, that breaks per-channel awareness, at severity 4.

[Press] Hostaway can hold different values per channel, but only field by field. And it shows that in seven different ways: an Edit per channel link, a scope caption on its own, an All channels label, a field that belongs to one channel, a row per listing, a section per channel, and a channel column. That breaks consistency and standards, a 3. There’s also a trap. Opening Edit per channel just to look marks the form as changed. So a host who only looked, then saved, has created overrides.

[Press] Exactly one screen, Instant book, treats the channel as a column. So the product already knows the pattern, and it works for every channel.

[Press] So in Smart Listings, each channel gets its own pages. Every field there is one of four cases. It uses the Base. It’s custom for that channel. It exists only on that channel. Or the channel sets it itself, like the description Booking.com writes. That one is read-only, with a lock that says why. Looking never changes anything. About 50 seconds.

DiscoveryData analysis

We want to see where listing trouble costs hosts and deals

Five sources, five questions.

SoIt sets what tips must cover.

Why data first

This is the data analysis the design specifies: what to pull, and the questions it must answer. Hostaway’s own teams hold the data, so we define the pulls and leave the answers open.

Five sources, each tied to a question. Field override usage shows which fields hosts override, and whether any of them is a text field. Support tickets show how often the assistant contradicts the listing, and what the edit questions cost. Sales calls show whether trouble grows with portfolio size. The public board shows what hosts ask for without being asked.

[Press] Those answers set what the tips must cover. We put data before hosts because exports need no recruiting, and they sharpen what we ask people. The alternative was to interview first, which risks asking hosts what the tickets already say.

Booking.com and Hostaway help pagesHow we score

We turned our thesis into hypotheses. Each one says what would prove it wrong, and we scored them by importance and evidence. These are the seventeen that decide Smart Listings. One was refuted before research began: write-back to Booking.com. Hostaway’s help centre and Booking.com’s partner docs showed that Booking.com writes the description itself.

[Press] So it left the list and became a design rule. On Booking.com, a host’s voice is the quality of their facts.

[Press] What we test first: does a why, a source and an undo make hosts trust AI tips? Who wants a different voice on each channel? What will hosts let Autopilot touch? Do they accept that on Booking.com their voice is their facts? And do they read the Hub as everything true about their home? Almost nobody asks for AI listing copy in public, so that demand is a bet too. The research design tests these first. About 50 seconds.

Who to talk to

This is the research design, built for a team to run. A prep week re-scores the assumptions, opens data access and starts recruiting. Week one is data first, because exports need no recruiting. That means sales calls from Gong, support tickets, how hosts use field overrides and the Knowledge Hub today, and ten interviews with Hostaway’s own teams.

[Press] Hosts come second, sharpened by what the data shows. About twenty-five user interviews over two weeks. Each stage answers its own question, written on its card. Week four is synthesis and the readout.

The first questions go out on day one. Is the Booking.com text an API limit, or Hostaway’s choice? What are Airbnb’s write limits? How can Hostaway spot drift? What Integrations or Legal would say is not guessed here. Those stay as nineteen open questions, each with an owner. We put data before hosts because the order saves host time. Hosts first would feel faster but risks asking what the data already answers. Access to Hostaway’s data and teams sets the order. About 55 seconds.
