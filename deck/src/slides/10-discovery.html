I started by mapping property management end to end. I used a real trial account, with a real Airbnb listing in Berlin. Not the whole product, just the part where a host sets up and runs a property. Everything hangs off the property: ten sections, in the product’s own menu order, from Overview to Direct.

[Press] The listing, the thing a guest reads, isn’t one of those sections. Each connected channel, like Airbnb, Booking.com or Direct, is a row on Overview. None gets a page of its own.

[Press] What can differ per channel lives inside the sections. Details, Pricing, Availability, Booking policies and Guest experience each have their own channel settings, field by field.

[Press] So each channel’s listing gets its own pages, with tips on every channel. The full map, every section with its pages, and how I made it, are in the detail. About 40 seconds.

This is the slide the case rests on. So I made sure it’s true for any host, not just my trial account. Next to every listing, Hospitable keeps a Knowledge Hub. Today it repeats the listing. The amenities, the rules and check-in are written again as separate entries, each with one source and a date. In my heuristic evaluation, that breaks source-of-truth clarity, at severity 4.

[Press] The Hub is filled once, when the host presses Generate, from Details and past messages. Hospitable’s help centre says it reads Details only once. So later edits never reach it. It drifts by design, not by accident.

[Press] And Copilot answers from that copy, not from the listing. On my trial listing, Ask the AI told a guest the apartment is wheelchair accessible. No amenity says so, and no source is shown. That breaks AI transparency, also a 4.

[Press] Meanwhile, the questions guests really ask stay in the Inbox, and nothing learns from them. So the Hub has no job of its own. My answer: listing details live once, in the Listing base. The Hub reads them, adds what the listing can’t say, and its job becomes guests’ questions. The severities are my own judgement, so I mark them as assumptions. About 60 seconds.

Product auditSeverity 0 to 4, one evaluator

The second gap in the structure is the channel view. Say a host asks the simplest question: what does my Airbnb listing say right now?

[Press] To answer it, they hop across five sections: Details, Pricing, Availability, Booking policies and Direct. They check about fifteen fields, one info icon at a time. And at the end, no page shows the answer. In the heuristic evaluation, that breaks per-channel awareness, at severity 4.

[Press] Hospitable can hold different values per channel, but only field by field. And it shows that in seven different ways: an Edit per channel link, a scope caption on its own, an All channels label, a field that belongs to one channel, a row per listing, a section per channel, and a channel column. That breaks consistency and standards, a 3. There’s also a trap. Opening Edit per channel just to look marks the form as changed. So a host who only looked, then saved, has created overrides.

[Press] Exactly one screen, Instant book, treats the channel as a column. So the product already knows the pattern, and it works for every channel.

[Press] So in Smart Listings, each channel gets its own pages. Every field there is one of four cases. It uses the Base. It’s custom for that channel. It exists only on that channel. Or the channel sets it itself, like the description Booking.com writes. That one is read-only, with a lock that says why. Looking never changes anything. About 50 seconds.

This is the data analysis, and it hasn’t run yet. I had no Hospitable data, so this is the plan: what I’d pull, and the questions it must answer.

Five sources, each tied to a question. Field override usage shows which fields hosts override. Support tickets show how often the assistant contradicts the listing, and what the edit questions cost. Sales calls show whether trouble grows with portfolio size. The public board shows what hosts ask for without being asked.

[Press] Those answers set what the tips must cover. The one real number is 618 calls across 457 accounts, from the brief. Everything else is planned.

Booking.com and Hospitable help pages

I turned my thesis into hypotheses. Each one says what would prove it wrong, and I scored them by importance and evidence. These are the seventeen that decide Smart Listings. One was refuted before research began: write-back to Booking.com. Hospitable’s help centre and Booking.com’s partner docs showed that Booking.com writes the description itself.

[Press] So it left the list and became a design rule. On Booking.com, a host’s voice is the quality of their facts.

[Press] What I test first: does a why, a source and an undo make hosts trust AI tips? Who wants a different voice on each channel? What will hosts let Autopilot touch? Do they accept that on Booking.com their voice is their facts? And do they read the Hub as everything true about their home? Almost nobody asks for AI listing copy in public, so that demand is a bet too. Three of my first five riskiest bets were about import. They wait for the next release. About 50 seconds.

This is the plan I’d run with a team. A prep week re-scores the assumptions, opens data access and starts recruiting. Week one is data first, because exports need no recruiting. That means sales calls from Gong, support tickets, how hosts use field overrides and the Knowledge Hub today, and ten interviews with Hospitable’s own teams.

[Press] Hosts come second, sharpened by what the data shows. About twenty-five user interviews over two weeks. Each stage answers its own question, written on its card. Week four is synthesis and the readout.

The first questions go out on day one. Is the Booking.com text an API limit, or Hospitable’s choice? What are Airbnb’s write limits? How can Hospitable spot drift? I simulated the host side to show the method. I did not simulate what Integrations or Legal would say. Those stay as nineteen open questions, each with an owner. About 55 seconds.
