This slide is the research design, not a report of work done. The question is this: do hosts vary money and stay rules per channel, or only the words? To answer it the study uses 25 user interviews, including hosts who left or never joined after a trial. It pairs them with data: about 600 sales calls to review, and 300 support tickets to tag.

[Press] The public sources are where we start: reviews and posts, channel concepts, and competitor products, each to be linked to its source in Notion.

[Press] The rule for a strong insight is three sources of two kinds, and one of them a host’s own words. We chose 25 interviews because that is about six per host type plus the hosts who left. We considered a survey alone. It is cheaper, but it cannot say why a host distrusts a change. The constraint is that hosts give little time, so each interview is paired with call and ticket data. The More panel has the same reasoning. The example findings on the next slides show what the output looks like. About 45 seconds.

We design for four host types. We gave each a name and a behaviour, and ordered them by experience, from Priya, new to property management software, to Callum, who runs a portfolio. They are example personas, not real people, and the hours are sample data. Priya wants to spend no time on listings at all. Marta spends about an hour a month on hers, and only when something breaks. Dana spends six to eight hours a month on her five units. Callum’s team spends about twenty hours a month on his portfolio.

[Press] In each persona the real listing lives somewhere else: Airbnb, a doc, a sheet. None of them treats Hostaway as the source. So the design does not declare Hostaway the source of truth. It makes every channel match Hostaway, and earns the trust one matching listing at a time. We considered one persona for all hosts, but the four differ most in how much time they give a listing, and that decides how much the assistant should do alone. About 45 seconds.

The synthesis ends in four root causes, and each pain below comes from one of them. First, the truth lives somewhere else: Airbnb, a doc or a sheet. So no single place is the one hosts trust. Second, per channel means more than words. Hosts may change the copy, but also the photos, the money and the stay rules. Third, a change lands with no preview and no proof. Fourth, the AI sounds surer than it is.

[Press] Each one leads to a product decision. One Base holds each fact once. Per-channel fields cover money and rules. Every change shows its reach before it’s saved, with a receipt after. Every suggestion says why, where, and how to undo it. The public source is Hostaway’s own feedback board, where hosts ask for what they need. About 55 seconds.

This replaces persona quotes with jobs. A job isn’t tied to a host type. Every host has the same goal: a listing that is right on every channel, and that they can check. So we built four jobs from the tool itself, its sections and its flows. The alternative was one job per persona, but that gives sixteen maps and hides what the personas share.

The channels’ own documentation, Booking.com as one exampleThe limits

Booking.com writes its own description from structured facts, so a host can only request a fix. That’s one example, not the rule. Each channel limits what Hostaway can write, and that shapes what it can push. Airbnb forks an edit made on the channel. Vrbo overwrites it. For Expedia we’ve read its supply docs: amenities and rules are fixed codes on each room type, and no free-text house rules.

[Press] So the design maps the limits for every channel: what Hostaway can write, how fast, and what the channel overwrites. For Expedia, who writes the description is an open question the design leaves visible. The answers become channel rules, shown across the product. We considered one generic rule set for all channels, but it would promise edits that a channel then drops or overwrites. About 45 seconds.

Every pricing tool we reviewed works the same way. PriceLabs, Beyond, AirDNA and Wheelhouse build a set of similar listings on the same channel. Then they put your nightly price against it: your rate, the median, and the middle half of the market. Four of the six market tools do this.

[Press] Nobody does that for the listing itself. The same twelve similar listings could show a host that their title is shorter, they have half the photos, and they name fewer amenities. Only AirDNA’s amenities list and Rankbreeze’s keywords come close, and only in part.

[Press] That’s the opening for the assistant. Read the similar listings and turn the gap into a tip, like leading with the living room photo. ResearchProblem statement

The problem: Hostaway gives hosts no listing they can trust

The problem

Hostaway keeps a home’s facts in two copies, varies the wrong things per channel, never says where a change went, and offers AI that persuades instead of being safe.

How might we…

  1. 1

    Two copies

    keep one record of the home that every channel and the assistant read?

  2. 2

    Wrong per channel

    give money and stay rules one per-channel pattern, and make voice a choice?

  3. 3

    Unseen changes

    show each channel’s listing, and say where every change went?

  4. 4

    Persuasive AI

    let the AI write only what the record can back?

The product auditOur synthesis of the example researchFour root causes

Here is the problem we’re solving, plainly. Hostaway keeps a home’s facts in two copies: the listing, and the Knowledge Hub the assistant answers from. It varies copy per channel, but hosts vary money and stay rules. It never says where a change went. And its AI tries to persuade instead of staying safe.

[Press] Each part becomes one how-might-we. One record of the home. One per-channel pattern for money and rules, with voice as a choice. Each channel’s listing, and where every change went. And an AI that writes only what the record can back. These became our design principles. About 45 seconds.
